📋 Contracts & Obligations

Business Law

The legal backbone of every sale, supplier deal, and delivery arrangement Kokorolls makes — even the ones sealed with nothing but a Viber message.

01Definition & Elements (OCAT)

Contract: an agreement between two or more parties that creates legal obligations. Philippine law requires four elements before it's binding: (1) offer and acceptance, (2) consideration, (3) intent to be legally bound, and (4) a lawful subject matter.
The OCAT rule
  • Offer: one party proposes specific terms to another.
  • Consideration: something of value changes hands — money, goods, or services.
  • Acceptance: the other party agrees to those exact terms.
  • Intent: both sides mean for legal consequences to follow.
At Kokorolls You list cinnamon rolls at ₱100/piece (offer). A customer pays ₱100 (acceptance + consideration). Both of you expect ownership to transfer and the sale to be final (intent). All four boxes checked — valid contract, even though nothing was signed.

02Types of Contracts

TypeDefinitionExample
BilateralBoth parties owe a performanceSale of goods — you deliver rolls, buyer pays
UnilateralOnly one party is obligated"₱500 reward if you find my missing delivery bag"
ExpressTerms stated clearly, oral or writtenA written agreement with your flour supplier
ImpliedTerms understood from conductA walk-in customer picks up a box and pays at the counter

03Obligations — What Each Party Must Do

Obligation: a legal duty one party owes another under a contract. There are three kinds.
  • To give: transfer ownership of something — you hand over the box of rolls.
  • To do: perform an action or service — a courier completes a delivery route.
  • Not to do: refrain from an action — a non-compete clause barring an ex-employee from opening a rival roll stall nearby.

04Breach of Contract & Remedies

Breach & the four remedies

Breach: one party fails to fulfill its obligation.

RemedyMeaningWhen used
DamagesMonetary compensation for lossesNon-breaching party needs to be paid back
Specific performanceCourt orders the breaching party to actually performMoney alone can't fix it — a one-of-a-kind item or property
RescissionContract is canceled, parties restored to original positionContinuing would be unfair to one side
ReformationCourt rewrites the contract's wordingBoth sides made the same honest mistake in drafting
Memory tipRemember OCAT — Offer, Consideration, Acceptance, Intent. Drop any one of the four and there's no valid contract at all, no matter how much everyone meant to be bound.

🤝 Agency

Business Law

Whoever collects payments, negotiates with suppliers, or manages a bazaar stall on your behalf is legally speaking for you — for better or worse.

01Principal & Agent Relationship

Agency: a relationship where one person (the agent) acts on behalf of another (the principal), with the principal's consent, in dealings with third parties.
At KokorollsYou hire someone to collect payment from bazaar customers on your behalf. That person is your agent; you're the principal. The customers are third parties dealing with your agent — but legally, you are the one bound by what the agent does.

02Types of Authority

Four ways authority can arise
  • Express: you say it directly — "you're authorized to collect payments," written or spoken.
  • Implied: reasonably necessary to carry out what was expressly authorized — collecting payment implies the power to issue a receipt.
  • Apparent (ostensible): looks like authority exists because of how you've represented the agent to others, even if you never actually granted it. You're still bound.
  • Ratification: you later approve of an unauthorized act, making it valid retroactively.

03Agent's Duties to Principal

An agent must:
  • Follow instructions that are lawful.
  • Act with care and diligence — reasonable skill and attention.
  • Act in good faith — honesty and fairness throughout.
  • Account for money and property received, and remit it promptly.
  • Avoid conflicts of interest — never profit at the principal's expense.
  • Keep information confidential — your recipe ratios stay yours.

04Principal's Liability for Agent's Acts

Key rule: a principal is liable for acts the agent performs within the scope of the agent's authority. If your agent signs a supplier contract on your behalf and within their authority, you are bound — the third party can hold you responsible even though the agent physically signed.
Memory tipAuthority in = liability in. The more authority you hand your agent, the more you're on the hook for what they do in your name.

🏢 Corporations

Business Law

If Kokorolls ever incorporates, the business becomes its own legal person — with its own risks separated from yours.

01Definition & Characteristics

Corporation: a legal entity created by law, separate and distinct from its owners (shareholders). It can own property, enter contracts, and sue or be sued — like a person of its own.
At KokorollsIf "Kokorolls by Vanie" incorporates, the business becomes its own legal person, separate from you. It can own equipment, sign supplier contracts, and answer for its own debts. Your personal house and car aren't automatically on the line if the business owes money.
Key characteristics
  • Separate legal personality — the corporation exists independently of its owners.
  • Limited liability — owners risk only what they invested.
  • Perpetual succession — it survives changes in ownership.
  • Transferable shares — ownership can be bought and sold.
  • Centralized management — run by a Board of Directors, not each owner individually.

02Types of Corporations in the Philippines

TypeCharacteristicsBest for
Stock corporationOwnership divided into shares; unlimited stockholders; profits distributed as dividendsBusinesses seeking investor funding or planning to scale
Non-stock corporationNo shares; members have equal rights; charitable, educational, religious, or civic purposeNGOs, schools, cooperatives, associations
One Person Corporation (OPC)A single stockholder who is also director and president; still enjoys limited liabilitySolo founders who want incorporation without needing co-owners

03Corporate Structure & Hierarchy

The chain of command

Shareholders → Board of Directors → Officers → Employees

  • Shareholders: own shares, vote on major decisions, elect the Board, receive dividends.
  • Board of Directors: sets policy, approves major decisions, hires and supervises the President/CEO.
  • President/CEO: runs day-to-day operations, implements Board decisions.
  • Officers & employees: execute operations under the CEO's direction.

04Limited Liability Shield — The Key Benefit

Key benefit: shareholders' personal assets are shielded from the corporation's debts. If the corporation owes ₱1,000,000 to creditors, creditors can go after corporate assets only — not your house, car, or personal bank account.
At KokorollsYou invest ₱100,000 to incorporate. Two years later the business owes ₱500,000 to suppliers. Creditors can seize business assets and equipment, but not your personal savings. Your maximum loss is the ₱100,000 you put in.
Memory tip"Separate entity" = separate liability. A corporation loses your investment if it fails — but never your house.

👥 Partnerships

Business Law

The structure to know before bringing on a co-founder, a silent investor, or a one-off bazaar collaborator.

01Definition & Formation

Partnership: a voluntary agreement between two or more people to combine resources — money, skills, property — and share the profits or losses of a common business.
At KokorollsYou and a friend agree to start a catering line together. You contribute recipes and baking know-how; your friend contributes ₱50,000 capital. You agree to split profit and loss 50-50. That's a partnership — you're both partners with equal rights unless you agreed otherwise.

02Types of Partnerships

TypeDefinitionLiability
General (GP)All partners manage and share profit/lossUnlimited — personal assets are exposed
Limited (LP)Mix of general partners (manage) and limited partners (invest only)Limited partners: liability capped at investment. General partners: unlimited
Joint ventureTemporary partnership for one specific project, dissolves afterDepends on the agreement

03Partnership vs. Corporation

AspectPartnershipCorporation
Legal entityGenerally not separate from partnersSeparate from shareholders
LiabilityPartners personally liable, unlimitedLimited to investment
ManagementAll partners, usuallyBoard of Directors
ContinuityDissolves if a partner leaves or diesContinues regardless of ownership changes
TaxationPass-through — partners taxed on their shareTaxed at the entity level, then again on dividends

04Rights & Duties of Partners

Rights
  • Share in profits per the agreement (or equally, if silent).
  • Participate in management decisions.
  • Inspect the partnership's books and records.
  • Get back their capital contribution on dissolution.
Duties
  • Act in good faith toward co-partners.
  • Contribute the agreed capital, skills, or property.
  • Share losses proportionally, unless agreed otherwise.
  • Not compete with the partnership for personal gain.
  • Not use partnership property for personal purposes.
Memory tipUnlimited liability = personal risk. In a partnership, a ₱1M business debt can reach your personal assets. In a corporation, you cap your loss at what you invested.

💰 Income Tax

Taxation

Collected by the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) on what individuals and corporations actually earn — not on everything that passes through the register.

01Types of Income

Income typeDefinitionExample
CompensationSalary, wages, bonuses from employmentA day-job salary of ₱25,000/month
Business incomeProfit from self-employment or business operationsKokorolls revenue ₱100k minus expenses ₱50k = ₱50k taxable
Capital gainsProfit from selling an asset or propertyBuy an asset at ₱100k, sell at ₱150k = ₱50k gain
Passive incomeIncome from investments — dividends, interest, rentA payout from an ETF holding
Other incomePrizes, winnings, miscellaneous gainsContest prize money tied to your business

02Allowable Deductions

Basic formula: Gross Income − Allowable Deductions = Taxable Income

Common deductions for a business owner:

  • Cost of goods sold — flour, sugar, butter, cream cheese, cinnamon, packaging.
  • Business supplies — labels, boxes, wrappers, utensils.
  • Rent and utilities — kitchen space, electricity, water, gas.
  • Depreciation of equipment — ovens, mixers.
  • Business insurance.
  • Wages paid to helpers or employees.
  • Professional fees — accountant, legal consultation.
  • Marketing and advertising — social media ads, promo materials.
Kokorolls monthly example Total revenue: ₱100,000
Less cost of goods sold: −₱40,000
Less utilities, packaging, delivery: −₱10,000
Less equipment depreciation: −₱2,000
Taxable income: ₱48,000 — you're taxed on ₱48,000, not the full ₱100,000.

03Tax Rates — Individual & Corporate

Taxable income (annual)Tax due
₱0 – ₱250,0000% — exempt
₱250,001 – ₱400,00015% of the excess over ₱250,000
₱400,001 – ₱800,000₱22,500 + 20% of the excess over ₱400,000
₱800,001 – ₱2,000,000₱102,500 + 25% of the excess over ₱800,000
₱2,000,001 – ₱8,000,000₱402,500 + 30% of the excess over ₱2,000,000

The 8% flat-tax option: self-employed individuals and professionals earning ₱3,000,000 or less a year can elect an 8% flat tax on gross sales (in excess of ₱250,000) instead of the graduated table above and instead of percentage tax. One number, no need to track itemized deductions — but you give up deducting your actual costs, so it only wins if your real expenses are modest relative to revenue.
Corporate income tax
  • 25% standard rate on net taxable income.
  • 20% preferential rate for domestic corporations with net taxable income ≤ ₱5,000,000 and total assets ≤ ₱100,000,000 (excluding the land the business sits on) — most small and micro corporations qualify.
  • Minimum Corporate Income Tax (MCIT) of 2% of gross income applies from a corporation's 4th year of operation onward, whenever it would otherwise owe less than that.
At KokorollsIf Kokorolls incorporates and taxable income is ₱48,000/month (₱576,000/year), it falls well under the ₱5M threshold, so the 20% MSME rate applies: ₱576,000 × 20% = ₱115,200/year, not the standard 25%.

04Filing Requirements & Deadlines

Filing deadlines & procedures

Individuals (self-employed / business owners):

  • Annual Income Tax Return (ITR): due April 15 of the following year.
  • Keep receipts, invoices, and business records for at least 3 years.
  • Quarterly income tax returns if business income is significant.

Corporations:

  • Annual ITR: within 15 days from the statutory deadline set for the corporate return following fiscal year-end (typically the 15th day of the 4th month after FYE).
  • Maintain a balance sheet, profit & loss statement, and cash-flow records.
  • Quarterly returns if gross receipts are substantial.
Memory tipGross ≠ taxable income. You're taxed on profit after legitimate deductions, not on total revenue — good records are what makes that difference count.

🛍️ VAT & Percentage Tax

Taxation

Two different consumption taxes, and which one applies depends almost entirely on your annual gross sales — this is the section that decides Kokorolls's actual tax treatment today.

01How VAT Works

VAT (12%): a consumption tax applied at each stage of production and sale. Businesses collect it from customers and remit the "value added" portion to government; the final consumer ultimately bears the cost.
Full VAT flow — flour supplier → baker → customer Stage 1 — supplier sells flour to you: ₱1,000 + ₱120 VAT = you pay ₱1,120; supplier remits ₱120.

Stage 2 — you sell rolls to a customer: ₱5,000 + ₱600 VAT = customer pays ₱5,600; you collect ₱600.

Stage 3 — your remittance: Output VAT ₱600 − Input VAT ₱120 = ₱480 remitted to BIR. You never keep the ₱600 — it's a pass-through, and the ₱480 you owe is the tax on the value you added.

02Input & Output VAT

Input VAT: VAT you paid on business purchases — creditable against what you owe.

Output VAT: VAT you collected from customers — this is what you must remit.

Net VAT payable = Output VAT − Input VAT. If Input exceeds Output, you can carry the excess forward or claim a refund.

03VAT-Able vs. VAT-Exempt Transactions

VAT-able (12%)VAT-exempt
Sale of goods and services generallySale of raw, unprocessed foodstuffs (rice, fish, vegetables)
Rental of property or equipmentAgricultural and aquaculture inputs and services
Professional servicesEducational services
Transportation servicesMedical and health services
Restaurant and cooked-food salesBank services, insurance premiums
For a bakery: baked goods are cooked, processed food — not raw foodstuffs — so they're VAT-able in principle. Whether you actually charge VAT depends on whether you're VAT-registered, which the next section covers.

04Percentage Tax & the 8% Option — the Realistic Answer for Small Businesses

The threshold that decides everything: under the TRAIN Law, VAT registration is mandatory only once annual gross sales or receipts exceed ₱3,000,000 in any 12-month period. Below that, you're a non-VAT taxpayer by default.
Non-VAT businesses instead pay one of two things:
  • Percentage tax (3%) on gross sales/receipts under Section 116 of the Tax Code — a single flat rate, no input/output tracking.
  • The 8% flat income tax option (see Income Tax) — if you elect it, it replaces both the graduated income tax table and percentage tax. You can't owe both.
You may also voluntarily register for VAT below the ₱3M threshold — useful if your buyers are VAT-registered companies wanting to claim input VAT from you — but doing so locks you into the VAT system for 3 years.
At KokorollsA home-based bakery doing bazaar and pre-order sales almost certainly earns well under ₱3M a year. That means Kokorolls is not required to register for VAT or charge 12% VAT on cinnamon rolls at all — the real obligation is either the 3% percentage tax or the 8% flat option, whichever nets out lower. This is the single most exam-relevant, business-relevant fact in this whole section — don't let the VAT walkthrough above make you think VAT is automatically Kokorolls's problem.

05VAT Registration & Filing

Registration requirements & filing schedule

Who must register:

  • Gross sales/receipts exceeding ₱3,000,000 in any 12-month period.
  • Importers, regardless of sales volume.
  • Providers of international/export services above the same threshold.

Process:

  • Register with the BIR and obtain a TIN, if you don't already have one.
  • Get your VAT Certificate of Registration.
  • File VAT returns — the current framework centers on quarterly filing, following the Ease of Paying Taxes Act's simplification of the old separate monthly and quarterly system.
Memory tipVAT at every stage, but nobody keeps it — the tax flows upward through the supply chain to government, and only the final consumer truly bears the cost.

🚗 Excise Tax

Taxation

A selective tax, not a general one — it targets specific goods rather than sitting on top of almost everything the way VAT does.

01What Gets Taxed

Excise tax: levied on specific goods considered luxury, non-essential, or harmful. Unlike VAT, it applies only to certain products, and it serves two purposes at once — revenue, and discouraging consumption.

02Excise Tax Categories & Rates

CategoryRatePurpose
Cigarettes/tobaccoSpecific peso rate per pack, adjusted annuallyRevenue + public health
Alcoholic beveragesVaries by type and alcohol contentRevenue + reduce excessive use
Petroleum productsPeso-per-liter, varies by fuel typeRevenue + environmental impact
Sugar-sweetened beveragesPer-liter rate, higher for purely sugar-sweetened drinksPublic health — anti-obesity, diabetes prevention
High-end automobilesProgressive, based on price/engine capacityRevenue, luxury tax

Exact peso rates shift with annual indexation and new revenue regulations — treat the categories and logic as exam-stable, but verify current peso figures against the latest BIR issuance before quoting a number.

03Excise Tax vs. VAT

AspectExcise taxVAT
Applies toSpecific goods onlyAlmost all goods and services
PurposeRevenue + regulation of harmful goodsGeneral revenue
RateOften very high on sin goodsUniform 12%
BaseUsually manufacturer/import priceEach transaction value along the chain
Can they overlap?Yes — a taxed item like cigarettes carries excise tax and VAT on top

04Administration

  • Administered by: the Bureau of Internal Revenue.
  • Who pays: manufacturers and importers compute and remit at the point of manufacture or import.
  • Separate from VAT: both can apply to the same product.
  • Rates change: via legislation, especially for fuel and "sin" products.
Illustration: a ₱100 pack of cigarettes might carry ~40% excise tax (₱40) remitted by the manufacturer, who nets ₱60. The retailer then sells at market price, and the consumer ultimately pays it all.
Memory tipExcise = selective. VAT applies to nearly everything; excise only to sin goods and luxuries, at rates steep enough to actually discourage use.

📊 Other Business Taxes

Taxation

The smaller, easy-to-forget taxes and exemptions that round out a small business's compliance picture.

01Local Business Tax

What: an annual tax imposed by the city or municipality on the privilege of doing business there, under the Local Government Code — separate from national BIR taxes.

Who pays: every business operating within the locality, whether profitable or not.

How it's computed: local government units set their own schedule of rates based on gross receipts or capital, within caps set by the Local Government Code, so the exact amount varies by city or municipality.
At KokorollsWhatever city or municipality your kitchen operates from will have its own barangay clearance and business permit fees, renewed annually — budget for these alongside your BIR obligations, since they're a separate government layer entirely.

02BMBE Exemption — Worth Checking For a Business Like Kokorolls

Barangay Micro Business Enterprises (BMBE) Act, RA 9178: a business with total assets of ₱3,000,000 or less (excluding the land it sits on) can register as a BMBE with the local government. Registered BMBEs are exempt from income tax on income from the business's operations, and may also get simplified local tax treatment and easier access to credit and technical assistance.
At KokorollsA home-based bakery with modest equipment (ovens, mixers) almost certainly stays well under the ₱3M asset ceiling. Registering as a BMBE — a one-time application at the city or municipal treasurer's office — could mean owing no income tax on Kokorolls' profits at all, on top of whatever percentage tax or 8% flat option applies. This is worth an actual conversation with an accountant, not just an exam fact.

03Real Property Tax

Assessed value: based on property classification, location, and market value set by the local assessor.

Rate: typically 0.5%–2% of assessed value, varying by municipality and property class.
Scenario: a kitchen/shop assessed at ₱500,000 with a 1% local rate means ₱5,000/year in real property tax.

04Documentary Stamp Tax

What: a tax on documents and instruments — contracts, deeds, leases, bills of lading — executed in the Philippines.

Rate: generally ₱1.50 per ₱200 of the document's value (or fraction thereof), with different rates for specific document types.

Paid by: the person executing or receiving the document.
Example: signing a ₱100,000 supplier contract triggers documentary stamp tax computed on that amount.

05Withholding Taxes & Capital Gains Tax

Withholding tax: tax the payor deducts before paying the payee, then remits directly to the BIR.
  • On compensation: employer withholds a graduated amount based on salary bracket.
  • On contractor payments: businesses commonly withhold around 5% when paying independent contractors or suppliers.
  • On payments to non-residents: royalties, management fees, rentals to foreign persons.
  • Final withholding tax: the withheld amount is the full and final tax — nothing more is due on that income.
At KokorollsYou hire a freelance designer for ₱10,000 to design labels. You withhold 5% (₱500), pay the designer ₱9,500, and remit the ₱500 to BIR.
Capital gains tax: on profit from selling capital assets.
  • Stocks sold on the exchange: 0.5% of gross selling price.
  • Real property: 6% of gross selling price (or fair market value, if higher).
  • Other capital assets: generally added to ordinary taxable income and taxed at the regular graduated rates, rather than a flat percentage.
Investment example: shares bought for ₱100,000, sold six months later for ₱150,000 — the ₱50,000 gain is what gets taxed, not the ₱150,000 sale price.
